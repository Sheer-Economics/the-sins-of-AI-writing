# The Sins of Writing with AI

Over the past couple of days, I have stumbled upon two separate articles that
provoke debate over the sins of writing with AI.

The first article is a
[report](https://www.independent.co.uk/arts-entertainment/books/features/jerry-falade-book-deal-ai-racism-b3050738.html)
of a novelist who allegedly lost a 2-million-dollar deal because publishers
could not be fully confident that the novelist's manuscript was free of AI writing.

The second article, from
[Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49767937), is a plea against
substantive use of AI in writing.
The author lists three main reasons:

<!-- prettier-ignore -->
> (1) the writing process is an essential part of the thinking process,
> (2) AI writing is vague and wrong in hard-to-notice ways, and
> (3) writing with AI is rude and misleading.

While I agree with the reasons, I would still argue that they do not logically
lead to a plea against AI.
A more defensible conclusion from that article is that caution is warranted
because AI is still immature, or that current uses of AI are primitive.
This is an increasingly contentious issue as AI permeates our lives, so I want
to analyze it from an economic perspective.

## Who is the culprit

AI is the new buzzword and easily enters the spotlight, becoming the focus of
criticism.
Is AI a tool or a species comparable to humans?
For now, I see AI as a tool, a more powerful tool than any other kind in human
history.
Once we replace the buzzword "AI" with "tool," it becomes more apparent that
the culprit is never the tool but the human who uses it.
The history of technology is full of new tools meeting doubt&mdash;almost
every time a new tool emerged, people cast suspicion on it.

In 1966, math teachers
[protested against calculator use](https://miroslao7.medium.com/chatgpt-wont-take-your-job-away-a96d9a7b6a8).
Their claims certainly bear merit, as modern mathematics education frameworks
heavily emphasize number sense for junior students.
However, the protest did not stop the use of calculators, which are integrated
into studies after Grade 9.
This historical parallel gives today's complaints about AI writing an air of
nostalgia.
Both the protest and the current complaint aim at the wrong target.

## Will AI be stopped 

People will always try to find an economical way of doing business, such as
cutting costs or improving products.
Resistance to technological advancement is perilous.
That advancement is the source of economic growth.
Therefore, efforts to go against the trend of technological innovation will be
in vain.
The usual strong pushback stems from people whose interests have been
negatively impacted by the emerging technology.
It's much like the British
[Red Flag Act](https://law-school.open.ac.uk/blog/red-flag-act) of 1865, which
required any self-propelled road vehicle to be preceded by a person walking at
least 60 yards ahead carrying a red flag.
This law was abolished in 1896; a similar act, passed in the U.S. in 1894, was
repealed two years later.
A better option, even for people whose livelihoods are disrupted, is to adopt
it rather than resist it.
The earlier the adoption, the better, because early adopters face less
competition.

## How to mute the AI-generated content

I agree with the three reasons listed in the second article.
Are they enough to warrant an exile of good writing?
The cost of AI-generated content is low, while the cost of separating it from
human writing is much higher.
This imbalance of costs is a real concern because the noise may overpower
human voices.

Fortunately, this is not the first challenge of this kind that humans have
faced.
The law of demand and supply explains why: when the supply of AI-generated
content increases, its price falls; as human writing becomes relatively
scarcer, its price rises.
If everything were robotic in a technological utopia, any human writing would
be as precious as water in a desert.
As long as there is demand, human writing will not disappear, and neither will
AI writing.

People in the market will come up with many solutions that help differentiate
AI from human writing.
Those solutions include the same mechanisms, such as labeling and branding,
that enable us to buy good-quality milk with little effort.
For example, more platforms require disclosing the use of AI, with a specific
option for flagging AI use in reviews and ratings.
Because of the rising risks of mistaking AI writing for human writing—as
the case in the first article shows—publishers may choose to work only
with authors who already have an established audience.
As a result, more authors choose self-publishing initially, bearing the costs
and benefits of their own writing.
Those are institutional arrangements that help prevent the abuse of AI
writing.

## The key question to ask

The rise of AI is driving a divergence among writers.
Some writers will use AI to enhance their writing without sacrificing their
uniqueness.
Some who replace independent thinking with AI will fall short in their
writing capacity.

AI has made previously impossible tasks economically feasible.
Suppose a Chinese author wants to translate his several niche nonfiction books
into English.
The economic bridge for such specialized translation did not exist before the
AI era.
He would need a translator who is not only fluent in both languages but also
an expert in that specific, narrow field—which makes the cost impossible to
justify for a small audience.
However, AI makes it affordable by enhancing the English writing of the
specialist or improving a Chinese-English translator's literacy in this niche
field.
The productivity improvement has become so substantial that a new role has
been created, _the human polisher_.
This human role is to polish the rigid AI translation and alter the text to
inject human rhythm or cultural elements.

Meanwhile, the problem of AI making students more dependent is growing more
evident.
There have been many reported instances of university students simply
copying and pasting without even checking the result, let alone thinking
independently.
Sam Altman said, "If we continue to teach students as if we were in a pre-AGI
world, it's gonna lead to an atrophy of critical thinking." When the new tool
changes people's behavior dramatically while the education system remains
stale, I would not be surprised to hear more of such stories.

For people who want to write exceptionally, AI can be their best assistant.
Being empowered by AI doesn't mean an easy job—the process can still be
strenuous, but it produces a far better outcome.
On the contrary, for people who just aim to finish the task with no concern
for quality, using AI or not makes little difference in terms of their
output.
In this case, using AI only makes them worse off in their capacity for
writing and thinking.

By using AI, are you aiming to excel, or just phoning it in?
This is the key question individuals should ask themselves.
And for education systems, the urgently needed change is to motivate students
to focus on the outcome, not just get homework out of the way.
